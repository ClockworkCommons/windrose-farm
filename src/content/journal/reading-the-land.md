Before cutting a single swale, we spent time with the NRCS Web Soil Survey —
and it did not flatter us.

The dominant soils here are the Leanto-Bisoodi complex and the
Mellenthin-Rock outcrop complex. In plain language: bedrock starts 8 to 20
inches down, water infiltrates very slowly, the soil is heavy with calcium
carbonate (very alkaline), and the top five feet of ground can hold roughly
an inch and a half of available water. Total.

It would be easy to gloss over this and post pretty pictures of seedlings.
But that number — 1.2 to 1.7 inches of available water — is the single most
important fact about this property, and pretending otherwise would make
everything else we share here less trustworthy.

## What it changed

The survey settled a debate we'd been having: do we improve the soil we
have, or build new soil on top of it? At these depths, the answer is mostly
the latter.

- **Hügelkultur mounds** become the backbone of the planting strategy —
  raised beds built from buried logs that hold moisture the native ground
  can't.
- **Contour swales** matter more, not less. When water can't sink in, it
  runs off — so we intercept it and give it time.
- **Plant selection** got stricter. Everything on the list — asparagus,
  jujube, cold-hardy apples, seaberry, lavender — earns its place by
  actually tolerating alkaline, shallow, dry ground.

## The quiet upside

The same survey work confirmed what the prickly pear and pinyon pine
already knew: things *do* grow here, on their own, with twelve inches of
rain a year and no help from anyone. The land isn't barren. It's just
honest about its terms.

Our job is to meet those terms, not fight them. Growing in nature's
direction — that's the whole idea.
